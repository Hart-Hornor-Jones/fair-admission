Fair to whom?

Whether an admissions test is fair depends on how you define fair. Six charts, built on a simulated pool of 100,000 applicants, show how reasonable assumptions can lead to contrary verdicts.

November 2026

Every argument about admissions testing appeals, sooner or later, to the value of fairness. Proponents say tests are fair because they predict college grades about equally well for everyone. Critics insist they are unfair because average scores differ between groups of students. Facts cannot settle such debate, which turns not on the nature of tests but the meaning of fairness.

Scholars have tried to pin it down. Cleary (1968), Thorndike (1971), Cole (1973), Darlington (1971) and Petersen and Novick (1976): a generation of psychometricians proposed definitions of fairness and devised methods for measuring it. Similar ideas have recently surfaced elsewhere, in the context of machine learning and algorithmic decision-making.

The six charts below show how different notions of fairness imply different judgments of the same situation. The situation includes a pool of applicants, an indicator of academic promise, a rule that selects on the indicator, and a desired academic outcome. What varies among charts is the standard of fairness they invite you to apply.

Chart 1An Imperfect Predictor

Admissions offices rank applicants on the basis of past achievements: grades achieved in high school courses, scores received on standardized tests, and so on. Their main concern, meanwhile, lies ahead, in a future still unknown. Their unenviable task is to wring information about the future from observations of the present.

The first chart below shows the relationship between an indicator (measurable in the present) and an outcome (to be realized later). It projects this relationship onto a simulated pool of 100,000 applicants. It represents each applicant as a dot. Its horizontal axis represents indicator values. Its vertical axis represents outcomes. The indicator is designed to resemble an indicator UC has found informative: a combination of high school GPA and SAT score. Values are standard scores centered on 50. The outcome is designed to resemble grade-point average.

The pool is simulated, but not invented. The share of applicants in each group, spread of grades and scores within each group, and association between indicator and outcome are determined by University of California reports. In aggregate, the pool closely resembles UC applicant pools described in the 2020 Standardized Testing Task Force Report.

What to trySwitch between the joint distribution and the two marginal views; the dots move. Click a group's name in the legend to hide it.

Notice: the cloud of dots is wide. At the same indicator values, students who would earn a 3.8 GPA line up with students who would earn a 2.2. The indicator cannot differentiate between those students. Notice also: the four groups differ more on the indicator than in what the indicator predicts. With the trend line on, hide groups one at a time and watch for shifting.

Chart 2A Selection Rule

An admissions office selects students by following a rule. Its decision to admit or deny follows, according to the rule, from each indicator value. Only those values -- and not the outcomes they predict -- are available at the time of the decision.

In the chart below, the selection rule is represented by a vertical line. Every dot to its right is an applicant admitted. Admitted applicants appear in the second plot, which represents the admitted class.

The rule can be defined in two ways: as a value on the indicator, or as a share of the applicant pool. The cut can be moved and also softened, such that odds of admission rise with proximity to the line.

What to tryDrag the cut score to fill or empty the class. Set the cut by applicant pool share to admit the top 40 percent, roughly UC's historical admit rate. Then soften or sharpen the cut.

Notice: the correlation within the admitted class is lower than the applicant pool's, and it falls further the stricter the cut. This is why correlations observed and reported tend to underestimate predictive validity. Universities can best observe the students they enroll, a deliberately biased sample.

Chart 3Costs and Benefits

Before addressing the issue of fairness, we need to define an object of value. Justly or unjustly, what can a university distribute? Many would answer: academic attainment. Universities develop and certify academic competence. Students receive, when admitted, opportunities to develop and demonstrate their academic abilities.

The third chart includes a new line: a horizontal bar whose level corresponds to GPA, set by default to 3.0. It represents a threshold, above which lies successful attainment; below, frustration. Together, the vertical and horizontal bars sort the applicant pool into four quadrants. Two of those quadrants represent errors:

  • In the applicant pool, above the attainment bar and left of the admission cut: students who would have succeeded but were turned away. This is the zone of untapped potential.
  • In the admitted class, below the attainment bar: students who were admitted but did not succeed. This is the zone of disappointment.

Above each subplot is a set of error rates, including one for the pool and one for each subgroup.

What to tryDrag the bars and watch the error rates change. Compare the two kinds of error. Compare rates among subgroups.

Notice how wasted potential is unevenly distributed: in groups with lower indicator scores, a larger share of would-be successes gets turned away.

Chart 4Make it fair: equal opportunity

Here is a reasonable standard of fairness: a student who would succeed should have the same chance of admission whatever group they belong to. Wasted potential should be no more common in one group than in another. This is Cole's (1973) standard; in the algorithmic-fairness literature it is called equal opportunity.

Adopt this standard of fairness. The next chart allows you to make admissions fair. You can turn three knobs. You can move the cut score. You can give bonus points to members of certain groups. And you can improve the indicator by making it more accurate.

What to tryFirst drag only the common cut-score. Try to achieve fairness in this way. Then give each group its own cut-score and lower the cut for groups that sit lower on the indicator. Try to achieve fairness in this way.

As you balance error rates of the first kind, consider error rates of the second kind. Fairness is gained at a cost. Equalizing opportunity requires admitting students from the lower-scoring groups at lower indicator values, and since the indicator predicts grades, a larger share of those admits will fall below the achievement bar. The second kind of error, disappointment, is now distributed unevenly across groups.

Chart 5Make it fair: same promise

Here is another reasonable standard, the one universities and courts have often invoked: an offer of admission should mean the same thing for members of different groups. If 90 percent of admitted students from one group go on to succeed, 90 percent from every other group should too. Disappointment should be no more common in one group than in another. This is the standard implied by Cleary's (1968) regression model, the one validity studies tend to measure. The algorithmic-fairness literature calls it sufficiency, or predictive parity.

The next chart is similar to the last, but subject to a different measure of fairness. What counted as fair in the previous chart doesn't qualify anymore.

What to tryTurn the separate cut-scores off, or drag them back together, and try to achieve fairness with one common cut-score. Then fiddle with the other knob, the indicator's accuracy, and try to achieve fairness that way.

Notice the cost this fairness requires. Fairness of this kind is gained at the other's expense. Whenever groups differ in their average indicator score, and the indicator is an imperfect predictor, no rule can simultaneously equalize opportunity and equalize promise. The two standards are compatible only when the indicator is perfect, which it never is, or when the groups do not differ, which is not the case in any existing applicant pool.

Chart 6Make it rational: optimal utility

Two standard definitions of fairness are mutually exclusive. Decision theorists of the 1970s (Gross and Su 1975; Petersen and Novick 1976; Sawyer, Cole and Cole 1976) attempted to solve this paradox by changing the question. Instead of asking about fairness, they asked about utility.

Suppose a student's success yields benefits and that such benefits can be quantified. Suppose likewise for a promising student's admission. Suppose failure, on the other hand, is costly, and such costs can be quantified. Likewise, the cost of wasted promise. Allow costs and benefits to vary with social groups: the cost of wasted promise or conspicuous failure may depend on whose promise and whose failure.

Once these quantities are decided, the verdict is unambiguous. An admissions process is good when it yields as much benefit as it can. The next chart allows you to maximize expected gains.

What to tryLeave the values at their defaults and press "Find the best cut(s)." Then raise the cost of turning away a student who would have succeeded, press again, and watch the cut move left. Then give one group a multiplier of 2 and turn separate cuts on: its cut moves on its own.

HSGPA is capped at 4.4 and SAT at 1600. Within each group HSGPA and SAT correlate 0.45, which gives a pooled correlation near the 0.53 (R² = 28%) the report gives among applicants.

First-year grades. Each student's first-year UC GPA is built from the regression UC's Institutional Research office fit to the 2015 entering cohort (IRAP 2020, Appendix C): standardized coefficients 0.23 on HSGPA and 0.36 on SAT, 25% of variance explained. Those are converted to raw units with enrollee standard deviations of 0.31 (HSGPA), 155 (SAT) and 0.56 (first-year GPA, backed out of the 2003–06 UCOP regression tables), so one HSGPA point is worth about 0.42 grade points and 100 SAT points about 0.13. Each group then gets the small offset from the common line that Geiser and Studley (2001, Table 8) measured for the HSGPA + SAT I model (last column above), plus normal noise with SD 0.485. The intercept is set so that the 60% of applicants admitted on a holistic reading have a mean first-year GPA of 3.05. Rather than a hard cap at 4.0, which would pile students on the ceiling, grades approach 4.0 through a soft ceiling (4 minus a softplus of slope 4 applied to the latent grade), so the distribution thins out toward 4.0 as real grade distributions do; the floor is 0.5. Nothing is fit to the outcome correlations directly; they are what the model produces:

The "top 60%" column keeps the 60% of applicants highest on a holistic reading that correlates 0.7 with the combined indicator, standing in for everything else in a file; a relationship measured in that narrowed pool reads weaker than in the applicant pool, so this is the column to compare with UC's reported figures. The combined indicator is the regression's own weighting of HSGPA and SAT, shown as a standard score with mean 50 and SD 10 over the pool. Nothing here is UC student-level data.

The rule. Admission depends on the indicator alone. With a sharp cut, everyone at or above the cut is admitted; with a softened cut, each applicant is admitted with a probability that is one-half at the cut and falls off below it (a logistic curve whose width is the sharpness setting, in indicator points). Each applicant carries a fixed lottery ticket, so softening the cut changes who is admitted smoothly rather than reshuffling everyone. Setting the cut by share of the pool draws it where that share begins; with a softened cut the share shown is the share at or above the cut, not the number admitted. Trend lines are fitted separately to the pool and to the class. Separate cuts per group are an offset from the common cut, which is the same thing as a point bonus. The predictiveness lever in charts 4–6 rescales the unexplained part of first-year grades so that the pool correlation takes the value shown; it changes grades, not the indicator.

The standards. "Succeeding" is clearing the chosen first-year GPA bar. Chart 4's standard is Cole's (1973) conditional-probability model, equal opportunity or separation on the positive class in the algorithmic-fairness literature: the false-negative rate among those who would succeed is the same in every group. Chart 5's is the standard behind Cleary's (1968) regression model, sufficiency or predictive parity: the share of admitted students who succeed is the same in every group. Each badge counts its standard as met when the largest gap between groups is five points or less, among groups with enough students to measure. Chart 6 is the expected-utility model of selection (Gross & Su 1975; Petersen & Novick 1976; Sawyer, Cole & Cole 1976): a value for each of the four outcomes, optionally weighted by group, and the cut that maximizes the expected total; because the total is a sum over students, with separate cuts each group's best cut can be found on its own.
