Costs and benefits

Missed and misplacedevery cut turns away students who would have succeeded, and lets in students who will not

Same pool, same rule. Now draw one more line: a bar across the grades that says what counts as succeeding in the first year. The cut and the bar together sort every applicant into four corners, and two of them are errors. In the pool, look above the bar and left of the cut: students who would have succeeded but were turned away. In the class, look below the bar: students who were admitted and did not succeed. Both counts are shown above the plots, for everyone and for each group, and both move as you move the cut. No cut makes them both zero; a stricter cut trades one for the other.

How this works

The pool is the one built in chart 1 from UC's published figures (see that chart's note). Admission depends on the indicator alone: with a sharp cut, everyone at or above the cut is admitted; with a softened cut, each applicant is admitted with a probability that is one-half at the cut and falls off below it (a logistic curve whose width is the sharpness setting, in indicator points). Each applicant carries a fixed lottery ticket, so softening the cut changes who is admitted smoothly rather than reshuffling everyone. The cut can be set either as an indicator value or as the share of the pool admitted; these are the same rule read from either end, since admitting the top share means drawing the cut where that share begins (with a softened cut, the share shown is the share at or above the cut, not the number admitted). The class plot shows the admitted students at exactly the indicator and grade values they had in the pool — the rule moves dots, it does not change them. Trend lines are fitted separately to the pool and to the class. "Succeeding" is clearing the chosen first-year GPA bar; the default of 2.5 is a C+ average, comfortably above UC's 2.0 good-standing line. The two error rates are, in the language of the fairness literature, the false-negative rate among those who would succeed (left) and the share of admits who do not succeed, one minus the positive predictive value (right).
